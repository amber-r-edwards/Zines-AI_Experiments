gridiron protest
                    by Rachel Scott

WASHINGTON: Women including WITCH will protest male chauvinism at the Gridiron Club Dinner at the Statler-Hilton Hotel on March 14.
     Publicized as a "strictly white-tie bluff affair", the dinner is given annually by the Gridiron Club, 50 of Washing- ton's most prestigious male Journalists. In the Club's 83rd list of important men, including President Nixon, Vice-President Agnew and the Supreme Court Justices.
     Women in the press and in poli- tics are not invited. For the most part, press women take a sour grapes attitude toward the affair. "We had convinced myself it probably wasn't worth missing," said the Washington Star. "They're so deadly boring."
     Agreed when one woman journalist said, "Besides, I've always felt we girls are picked last to play on the sandlots only so we can go fetch the balls."
     "It's not so much the Club dinner," said another woman, "It's just the way everything works in Washington. Fifty per cent of the city is female, but the ratio falls to a scant ten per cent by the time you reach top government jobs. You still have to be twice as smart as a man to make it in this town."
     But the women's movement is growing and the pressure for change is mounting in Washington DC as well as in more places than the South.

equal rights
amendment
         by Rachel Scott

WASHINGTON D.C.: Betty Friedan, President of NOW (National Organ- ization for Women) led a press con- ference on February 14, on the occa- sion of joint hearings of the House and Senate committees with delegations from Pittsburgh and Washington, D.C.
     Betty Friedan said, "No action since the fight for the Nineteenth Amend- ment and the vote for women is as important as the passage of the Equal Rights Amendment." 
    The proposed 26th amendment pro- vides: "Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridges by the United States or by any State on account of sex." Friedan said it was time to "stop giving women the piecemeal peanut butter approach to rights," citing the patchwork of State laws which restrict and limit women's rights.
     And the United States is the only country in the Western World not to have equal rights for women in its Constitution, shared Sara Evans, NOW member.
     For consideration of the question how discrimination really is, Friedan said: "If men had babies, child care would be on every corner." "We won't accept one single millimeter less than full constitutional equality for women."
     Today is the 50th anniversary of the 19th amendment, and we do not want another 50 years to elapse before political power is opened to the entire sex. "We don't want roses on Mother's Day, we want our rights under the Constitution."

women win
legal case

     Last September women won a case contesting the legality of state protec- tive laws which were being used by com- panies to keep women out of better jobs. Palmar Tirosi was fired by Colgate Palmolive when she tried to get a job which paid 35 pounds of sheets a requirement for all men jobs. When fired for this reason, she was one of 52 women let go. The National Organization for Women led the case in behalf of Tirosi. The court found the last acts of Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act: This act does not allow protective labor laws to be used to discriminate against women. The Federal court ruled against Colgate. The result of this decision is that women must have the equal ability to qualify for strenuous work and be given the same job tryouts for such jobs.
    The importance of this case is that it clearly shows up the male chauvinism used to keep women from good and better jobs. Protective labor laws were written in the 19th century, as a result of organizing and hard- fought strikes by working women. But protection for women has been used by companies to keep them from the better jobs. But not a single man was ever forced to leave a job "for his protection." The ruling is a precedent, a decision women will long try to enforce. For the future, NOW feels that the ruling has done much for women workers. The women who won feel it has represented them, and for all women in in- dustrial jobs.
